A matched case-control study of SEC enforcement cases

Do related-party transactions give fraud away before the SEC does?

Firms later charged by the SEC over a related-party transaction are compared with firms from the same industry and of similar size that were never charged, using only what each firm had already published. The observation window is the five fiscal years before the case became public; the misconduct is usually already under way in those years.

How the study works, in seven steps

  1. Research question. Can the financial, governance and disclosure characteristics of a firm's related-party transactions identify elevated fraud risk earlier than audit and internal-control signals do?
  2. Data collection. Public EDGAR filings only: annual reports, proxy statements, XBRL financial values and 8-K items.
  3. Case and control matching. Each case firm is matched with up to five firms of the same industry and size that had no enforcement; the five fiscal years before revelation form the window.
  4. Characteristic extraction. Eight characteristics are coded from the filings with the evidence sentence kept: four related-party amounts, two disclosure measures, two governance measures.
  5. Risk-score construction. Each characteristic is ranked 0–1 across the analysed firm-years, averaged within its layer, and the three layers averaged into a 0–10 score.
  6. Hypothesis testing. Five pre-registered hypotheses are tested within matched sets (conditional logit, Holm-adjusted, firm-clustered errors) and against the traditional audit signals.
  7. Findings. Reported as associations observed before public disclosure, with their uncertainty.

What this study finds

H1 financial exposure… H2 disclosure… H3 governance… H4 earlier years… H5 adds to audit signals…

Each dot is one analysed firm-year's rank-averaged risk score. The two distributions overlap substantially; the median of the case firm-years is only slightly higher, and the 95th-percentile threshold of the matched firms separates two of the 50 cases. The score does not distinguish the groups on its own.

Governance (CEO duality, board independence) is higher in case firm-years than in their matched firms with model-based standard errors; with the firm-clustered errors the design asks for, the evidence is not significant.

Disclosure runs the other way: case firms' related-party disclosures are, if anything, more complete and plainer than their matched firms'.

Financial exposure is tested only on the firm-years that state an amount, a subset in which case firms are over-represented; the association is not significant with firm-clustered errors.

The combined score adds nothing to the material-weakness, audit-opinion, auditor-change and restatement signals, in strength or in timing.
